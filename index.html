About

I am a computational scientist trained in physics, working at the interface of computational materials science, biophysics, and chemistry — currently as a postdoctoral research associate in the Joseph Lab at Princeton University. There, I use molecular simulation to study how small molecules partition into biomolecular condensates.

During my PhD, I used computational modeling of adsorption to study adsorption-induced deformation in porous materials, building on undergraduate research where I ran low-temperature nitrogen adsorption experiments to characterize nanoporous materials. That path — from the bench to the simulation — shapes how I now think about problems: I want to leverage computation to help bioengineering address disease-related challenges.

Research

Small molecules in biomolecular condensates

Postdoctoral research · Joseph Lab, Princeton

Biomolecular condensates are membraneless compartments that concentrate proteins and nucleic acids inside cells, and they are emerging targets for drug discovery. I study how small molecules partition into condensates using a multiscale simulation approach — spanning all-atom molecular dynamics with enhanced sampling, coarse-grained models to reach the length and time scales condensates demand, and quantum-mechanical calculations of solute electronic structure — to uncover the rules that govern partitioning and predict it from simulation.

I am extending this work using Bayesian optimization and machine-learning-assisted force field development, run on Princeton's Tiger HPC cluster. In progress.

JACS Au 2025 · Biophys. J. 2024

Long-timescale simulation on Anton 3

Molecular dynamics · D.E. Shaw Research

I won and now lead a Principal Investigator allocation on Anton 3, the special-purpose supercomputer built by D.E. Shaw Research for molecular dynamics. Anton 3's architecture is designed specifically to accelerate MD, letting me run large biomolecular systems out to timescales that would be impractical to reach on conventional hardware — access that shapes what questions about condensate dynamics and small-molecule partitioning are within reach.

Adsorption in nanoporous materials

Doctoral research · with Prof. Gennady Y. Gor

My doctoral work used molecular simulation, together with macroscopic theory and close collaboration with experimentalists, to understand how fluids behave when confined in pores only a few molecules wide — and how the pores themselves respond. The materials ranged from silica and carbon to zeolites and metal–organic frameworks, including the capture of hazardous chemicals: I studied the adsorption of the nerve agent sarin and its common simulants on metal–organic frameworks, taking a closer look at how the agent compares with the simulants commonly used in its place.

Adsorption-induced deformation

Fluids adsorbed in nanopores exert large solvation pressures that can strain the host solid. I combined macroscopic theory with molecular simulation to calculate solvation pressure in spherical mesopores, showing that it can reach hundreds of megapascals in small pores and that its value at saturation gives access to the solid–liquid surface energy. I extended this to zeolites 4A and 13X in a combined experimental and simulation study, and to the elastic properties of fluid-saturated zeolites.

AIChE J. 2021 · Langmuir 2023 · J. Phys. Chem. C 2025 · ACS Appl. Mater. Interfaces 2023

Force field development

Reliable adsorption predictions depend on reliable molecular models, so alongside my adsorption studies I develop and validate classical force fields — parameterizing and testing them against experimental thermodynamic data. I used this approach to study the vapor–liquid equilibrium of toxic organic compounds such as isocyanates, and contributed to a review of the thermodynamic signatures that nanoporous materials leave on confined fluids. This is the same force field development work I now extend, with machine learning, to drug-like molecules.

J. Phys. Chem. B 2021 · Book chapter, 2020
